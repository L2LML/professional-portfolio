# Closing the Data Gap: A DMAIC Case Study in Behavioral Incident Tracking

**Process map:** [`process-map.html`](./process-map.html) — self-contained, open directly in a browser, no server required.

A real DMAIC case study from direct-care work at Washtenaw ISD (Special Education), built as proof-of-skill for Business Process Analyst / Process Improvement roles. Unlike the [healthcare-burnout-dmaic](../healthcare-burnout-dmaic/) proposal, this is not a hypothetical applied methodology — it's a real 3-person team's process, mapped from an actual before-and-after with real numbers.

The map is a **cross-functional swimlane flowchart** (standard oval/rectangle/diamond/parallelogram notation, the same shape language Lucidchart or Visio would export) with one lane per role — Lisa, the other two paraprofessionals, and the BCBA — so it's explicit who performed which step. In the To-Be diagram, every step Lisa personally introduced (not just performed) is marked with a ★, separating her individual process-design contribution from the steady-state process the whole team ran afterward.

## Core thesis

A data-collection process can have every tool and every training session in place and still collapse — because access and knowledge gaps aren't the only failure mode. **A process only sustains itself if the people doing the work can see that the data leads somewhere.** This case study demonstrates that finding twice: once by fixing it, and once by watching an identical fix fail elsewhere for exactly the reason predicted.

## Structure (DMAIC)

| Phase | Content |
|---|---|
| **Define** | A BCBA (Board Certified Behavior Analyst) flags that behavioral data across the program is irregular and insufficient, and asks for more consistent collection. |
| **Measure** | Baseline observed directly: of a 3-person paraprofessional team, only 1 (33%) was collecting data regularly; the other 2 were not collecting at all. |
| **Analyze** | Three root causes identified: (1) tool/access friction — sheets weren't stored per-student, no writing surface, no pens; (2) a knowledge gap — one team member didn't understand the ABC recording method; (3) no feedback loop — data collected had no visible use, so it had no visible value. |
| **Improve** | Per-student clipboards (fixed access), pens physically attached (closed a friction point that surfaced *after* the first fix), a team + BCBA training meeting (closed the knowledge gap and aligned everyone to one standard), and a class/subject memory-anchor technique for staff who couldn't recall exact incident times. |
| **Control** | Daily dated turn-in with the collector's name recorded, filed into a single collection point (a red folder, front of classroom) — giving the process a defined location and owner, and making the BCBA's review of it a routine rather than an afterthought. |

## Result

- Team data-collection compliance rose from **33% to approximately 80%** over roughly two months.
- The BCBA's review of the resulting data identified a pattern for one student, leading to a schedule change (moving swimming to first period to help her regulate) that produced a **20% reduction in behavioral outbursts**.

## The comparison that validates the root cause

The same fix — accessible tools, training, dated turn-in — was applied to a parallel effort tracking IEP goal data with the same team. It reached 80% compliance in the first month, identical to the behavioral-data effort. But no one reviewed or acted on the IEP data afterward, and compliance fell to 30%, then under 10%, within two more months — including a drop in the case author's own recording frequency once it became clear the data wasn't being used. The two efforts differ in exactly one variable, which is what makes this a genuine (if informal) natural experiment rather than an anecdote: **the feedback loop, not the tooling or training, is what determines whether a process sustains itself.**

## Skills demonstrated

DMAIC methodology · root-cause analysis · 5S-style workplace/tool design · process mapping · stakeholder facilitation (cross-functional alignment with a clinical expert) · data-driven decision making · sustainment/control design · honest evaluation of a comparison case, including a result that didn't hold.
